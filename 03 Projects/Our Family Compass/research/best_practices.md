# What the research says, for this compass

Gathered 2026-11-17 by web search, as a first pass for Jimmy to learn from. Most links below were seen in search results and **not opened or read in full**. Each row says how strong the evidence looks from what was seen, so nothing here is settled. Check a source before quoting it in a piece.

Public repo: this file holds general research and Claude's thoughts about the *design*. It holds nothing about the family. Claude's understanding of the family, if it is ever written down, goes in a `private/` folder (ignored by git), not here.

## 1. Family stories and a child's sense of self (Library, Roots)

| What | Evidence, as seen |
|---|---|
| Emory researchers Marshall Duke and Robyn Fivush built the 20-question "Do You Know?" scale (where grandparents grew up, how parents met, the source of the child's name, hard times the family got through). Children who knew more tended to show higher self-esteem, a stronger sense of control, better family functioning and fewer behaviour problems. | Correlational. One study of 66 teenagers. Most of what I found was popular write-ups; the academic source named is Duke, Lazarus and Fivush, 2008, *Psychotherapy*. Not read. The exact item list differs between write-ups. |
| It is the telling between generations that seems to matter, not only knowing the answers. The proposed idea is an "intergenerational self": feeling part of something bigger. | Authors' interpretation, as reported. |
| Three family story shapes: ascending ("we rose"), descending ("we fell"), and oscillating ("we had ups and downs and stuck together"). The oscillating shape is described as the most helpful for resilience. | As reported in popular sources. |

Links: [Psychology Today, the 20 questions](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-stories-of-our-lives/201611/the-do-you-know-20-questions-about-family-stories) · [ParentMap, stories and resilience](https://www.parentmap.com/article/the-power-of-story-weaving-threads-of-resilience-across-generations)

**Claude's thoughts for the design.** Library pieces should include hard times and how they were met, not only successes, because the oscillating shape is the one reported as helpful. The 20 questions are a ready list of prompts for a "story waiting for its teller". Check the original list before using it.

## 2. Raising caring, ethical children (Principles, Workshop)

| What | Evidence, as seen |
|---|---|
| Harvard's Making Caring Common project: say that caring matters and hold high ethical expectations; model empathy; discuss ethical dilemmas from real life; widen the circle of concern; "zoom in" on people close by and "zoom out" to bigger ideas. | Practitioner guidance from a university project. Only snippets seen. |
| Their survey finding: a large majority of children see their parents as prioritising achievement or happiness over concern for others. | Reported in the same summaries. Not checked. |
| Explaining why (inductive discipline) is linked to higher moral reasoning. A short parent-training study found it can be taught. | Mostly correlational. The training study is a small thesis (8 and 9 year olds). Treat as preliminary. |
| Mammen, Köymen and Tomasello (2019): 4 and 6 year olds argued back and gave reasons more with peers than with their mothers. With a parent, moral statements can feel like fixed rules; with a peer they feel open to negotiation. | One study. Abstract seen only. |

Links: [Harvard MCC, 7 tips](https://mcc.gse.harvard.edu/resources-for-families/7-tips-raising-caring-kids) · [Harvard MCC, empathy](https://mcc.gse.harvard.edu/resources-for-families/5-tips-cultivating-empathy) · [Iowa State, induction (textbook chapter)](https://iastate.pressbooks.pub/parentingfamilydiversity/chapter/induction/)

**Claude's thoughts for the design.** (a) The "achievement over caring" gap is a reason to say plainly in Principles what ranks above achievement, and to show it in what the family does, not only says. (b) The peer result suggests moral games should often be played by the two children together, or with a grandparent, so they can disagree without a parent's rule hanging over it. Parents can then listen. (c) Every moral game ends with an open question, which fits the "explain, don't command" finding.

## 3. Letting a child choose (Open sky, "connected, not constrained")

| What | Evidence, as seen |
|---|---|
| Self-determination theory: parents who support a child's autonomy (explain reasons, offer choices, take the child's view seriously) rather than control them are linked to better motivation, adjustment and well-being. | A meta-analysis of the dual-process model reports an effect of about r = 0.20, which is small to modest. Associations, not proof of cause. Effects vary by age and by child. |
| Autonomy support looks different at different ages; it is not the same as letting a child do anything. | Developmental review (McCurdy, Williams and colleagues, 2020), seen as a listing. |

Links: [SDT, parenting topic page](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/topics/application-parenting/) · [McCurdy et al. 2020 (PDF)](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/2020_McCurdyWilliamsEtAl_Measurement.pdf) · [Soenens et al. 2015 (PDF)](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/2015_SoenensVansteenkisteVanPetegem_CDP.pdf)

**Claude's thoughts.** This supports the three-level idea: firm on level 1, explained on level 2, open on level 3. The effect is modest, so the case for it is mostly about what kind of relationship the family wants, not a promise of results.

## 4. Heritage, identity and language (Roots)

| What | Evidence, as seen |
|---|---|
| A 2017 systematic review (Juang and colleagues) of Asian American family socialisation: parents often stress ethnic heritage while promoting diversity, and different kinds of message relate to different outcomes. | Review named in a search summary. Not opened. |
| Mixes of messages seem to matter. One study of Asian American adolescents found the best academic and psychosocial outcomes with high ethnic identity and cultural socialisation but low preparation for bias. | Small sample of college students; suggestive only. |
| Cultural socialisation tends to be linked to positive outcomes; the effects of preparing a child for bias, or promoting mistrust, were mixed. | A conference abstract. |
| I did not find a meta-analysis aimed only at Asian American families. | |
| Li and Matthews (2022), "Turning the Tide", *Journal of Chinese Linguistics* 50(2), 291 to 322: in one three-generation Chinese American family, the birth of the third generation led the second-generation parents to take up Chinese again. Awareness of the benefits of bilingualism and of a Chinese American identity led them to pass it on. | The abstract matches the claim from the starting conversation. A single family case study, so it does not generalise. Full text not read. |

Links: [Greater Good, identity and well-being of Asian American youth](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_identity_shapes_wellbeing_asian_american_youth) · [Turning the Tide, journal listing (PDF)](https://cup.cuhk.edu.hk/image/catalog/journal/jpreview/JCL_50.2_291-322.pdf)

**Claude's thoughts.** The research agrees with "offered, never graded": heritage seems to help when it is warm and identity-building. It does not say that more insistence helps. Keep Roots pieces as invitations. Also note what is not known: how this holds for a family with a mix of backgrounds.

## 5. Discussion programmes, and being honest about results (Workshop)

| What | Evidence, as seen |
|---|---|
| Philosophy for Children (children discuss open questions together). A first trial found about two months' extra progress in maths and reading. A larger, later trial (NFER, reported 2021; 75 intervention and 123 control schools) found no effect on reading or maths, and no significant gain in character-related skills. | Two randomised trials; the larger one is null. The funder planned no further trial. |

Links: [EEF, effectiveness trial](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/philosophy-for-children-effectiveness-trial/) · [NFER report page](https://www.nfer.ac.uk/publications/evaluation-of-philosophy-for-children-an-effectiveness-trial/)

**Claude's thoughts.** Do not promise that Workshop games raise grades or build character in any measurable way. The reason to do them is the family's own: practising judgment together, and hearing each other. That is a fine reason, and an honest one. It also suggests the Compass should not count scores or ask children to perform.

## 6. Leaving values in writing (the whole project)

| What | Evidence, as seen |
|---|---|
| The "ethical will" or legacy letter is an old practice, with roots in the Jewish tradition and echoes in others. Advice that repeats across guides: tell stories that show a value being lived rather than listing values; keep it in your own voice, short; decide who it is for; do not write anger into it; treat each child equally. It is not a legal document. | Practitioner and tradition sources. No outcome research seen. |

Links: [My Jewish Learning, writing and reading ethical wills](https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/writing-and-reading-ethical-wills/) · [AARP, ethical will](https://www.aarp.org/caregiving/financial-legal/info-2018/ethical-will)

**Claude's thoughts.** This is close to what this project is, made living and shared. Two of its rules are worth adopting: values arrive through stories, and each child gets the same attention. Our own addition is the thing the ethical will cannot do: be revised, answered and disagreed with.

## 7. Growing up with AI and feeds (Workshop topic)

| What | Evidence, as seen |
|---|---|
| Practitioner guides in 2025 and 2026 agree on: teach checking (treat AI as a fast first draft, check answers together, deliberately look for mistakes); ask why a feed shows a thing; talk early and often; widen independence step by step; model your own habits. | Mostly practitioner and vendor articles. A Penn State guide warns that earlier social media research often assumed harm without good evidence, and the same could happen with AI. I found no consensus statement from a medical body. |

Link: [Penn State, navigating AI as a parent](https://thrive.psu.edu/?p=3304)

**Claude's thoughts.** Evidence here is thin and moving fast. A good Workshop piece teaches a *habit* (check, ask why, notice how you feel after) rather than a rule about a tool. Review any piece on this in a year.

## What this suggests for the framework

1. Add "hard times and how we met them" as a standing prompt in the Library.
2. Let children play moral games together before a parent is in the room, and keep their disagreement as a piece.
3. Keep level 1 short and explain level 2; leave level 3 open. That matches the autonomy research in kind, though not in size.
4. Say what ranks above achievement, and show it.
5. Be honest on every piece about what it is not (already a field).
6. Give each child the same attention (from the ethical-will guides).
