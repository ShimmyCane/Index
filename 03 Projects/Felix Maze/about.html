What this is

Felix Maze makes black-and-white maze sheets to print, in many styles: paintings, animals, machines, music, famous mazes from history. Every sheet has a title, a start and a finish with a character to follow, and one fun line at the bottom about where the idea came from. The aim is joy, not a test: lots of white space, a little story from start to goal, and something to notice on the way.

Each maze is a small recipe (a style, a size and a number called a seed), so the computer can rebuild the same maze, its answer and its animation exactly. Nothing is stored as a picture.

How mazes work

Maze or labyrinth?

A labyrinth has one winding path and no choices: you just walk it, like the floor of Chartres Cathedral. A maze has branches, so you choose, get things wrong and turn around. Most of ours are mazes; a few (the Cretan and Chartres rings, the turf maze) are labyrinths made to feel like mazes by adding branches.

A maze is a tree

Think of every square as a dot and every open doorway as a line between two dots. A "perfect" maze connects all the dots with exactly one route between any two, which is what mathematicians call a spanning tree. Walls are simply the lines we did not draw.

Growing one

Start in one square and keep carving into a neighbor not yet visited. Which square you extend next changes the feel: always the newest makes long winding halls with few branches; a random one makes many short, spiky branches. We call this slider twistiness.

Loops and bridges

Removing a few dead ends adds loops (more than one way, "braiding"). Letting a passage go over and under another makes a bridge, like the Celtic knot maze, where the answer slips under a path and comes out the other side.

Other shapes

Squares can be hexagons (six neighbors), rings and spokes (a spider web), or any silhouette: we only keep the squares inside a fish, a castle or a letter. Bent mazes (waves, spirals) are made flat and then pushed through a swirl.

Same seed, same maze

The random numbers come from a seed, so the maze is fully reproducible. A small fingerprint of each published sheet is checked on every change, so a maze that was printed cannot quietly change afterwards.

Try the dials

Change a dial and a new maze grows. Turn the twistiness all the way down for long halls, all the way up for spiky branches.

Levels, joy and checks

Level 1 to 5. The computer measures how long the right way is, how many choices there are along it, how deep the dead ends are and how tempting they look, and turns that into a level (level 1 is about a minute; from level 3 each level roughly doubles the time: 5, 10, 20 and now 40 minutes for level 6). The scale was set against a maze Jimmy knew was a level 2. The bank is a bell curve centered a little above 2:

Joy. A second score looks at three things: Looking (is it pretty, with room around it), Doing (do your hand and pencil have fun) and Solving (is the thinking satisfying, with wrong turns that are interesting rather than dull). Felix's faces (😴 😊 😣) after a try slowly move his "sweet spot" level.

Checks before anything is published. The red answer line must never touch a wall (first it is checked against the geometry, then against the pixels of the drawn picture, so what you see is what was checked). Only about 1 in 3,000 tries failed this, and failures never get out: they go on a list to be repaired. A layout check keeps the start and finish pictures off the title and footer. A sheet you flag with 🚩 is taken out of every pick (puzzle of the day, top five, packs) until it is fixed.

Art and inspirations

Many mazes are "inspired by" a famous painting, building or pattern. We use historical artists only, people who are no longer alive, so the mazes stay about their work and never imitate the style of artists working today. Nothing is traced: each maze borrows an idea (swirling sky, grid of rooms, dots, a cut-paper shape) and builds its own picture. The footer of each sheet says where the idea came from.

Ways to play

  • Print a pack: ten puzzles, double-sided, black and white, one per page.
  • 🚗 Drive a maze: on a tablet or computer, drag along the path. Your line is one color and turns green when it connects to the finish. You can turn around in dead ends; afterwards it shows where you looked and replays your drive. It does not have to be the shortest way.
  • 🎬 Answers: type a maze number to watch the way through.
  • ✏️ Make a maze: draw a few lines, pick a sprout, bake it into a maze.
  • 🚩 Flag: on any sheet or drive page, flag a maze that does not work for you (wrong-looking answer, too hard, too easy, ugly, unsuitable). It is hidden from the picks until fixed.
  • 🧪 Prototypes: ten ideas that only work on a screen: 3D tabletop, fly-through, globe, drum, donut, cube, shifting walls, spinning rings, tower, flood.

Keep exploring

Open questions

  • True organic mazes: curves that grow and push away from each other, not squares at all.
  • Mazes that depend on a rule (follow the color, count by fives, only one-way arrows).
  • Triangles, Truchet tiles, Hilbert curves as the maze's skeleton.
  • A maze you can only solve by turning the page, or in two halves (front and back).
  • Which prototype should become a real game? Which one made Felix laugh?
  • More historical art: Rembrandt's light, Gaudí's curves, Islamic geometry, Japanese woodblocks.
