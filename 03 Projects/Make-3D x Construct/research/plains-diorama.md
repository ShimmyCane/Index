# Research: the plains camp diorama (sample)

Purpose: the basic facts behind `samples/plains-diorama/`, what is known, what is not, and what must be checked before any display.
Scope: a generic plains camp in a science-diorama box, 1:24 (1 in = 2 ft). No specific nation, family or place is depicted.

## 1. Terms used

- The request said "planes". Read as **plains** (the Great Plains of Oklahoma and nearby states).
- The request said "Indians". This folder uses **Native nations** or the name of a specific nation where one is meant. "Indians" is the older term; use the nation's own name where it is known.

## 2. Why a diorama, and what the genre is

- Habitat dioramas (a painted backdrop, modelled ground and figures in a glass case) began with Carl Akeley's work in 1889, and were partly a tool for public support of conservation and education. (New Criterion review, see §8.)
- Native displays in these halls have drawn sustained criticism. Critics say placing Native people among taxidermy and fossils repeats the dehumanising framing of the period, and that the stories were written by curators, not by the peoples shown. (Springer chapter and reviews, §8.)
- The AMNH *Old New York* diorama (1939), of Dutch settlers meeting the Lenape, is the usual case study. It was reconsidered in 2018 to correct stereotyped portrayals. (§8.)
- **Implication for this sample:** the figures are placeholders, the camp is generic, and the diorama is not a model of any real people. Any version that names a nation needs its consultants before it is shown.

## 3. Oklahoma and the Plains nations (basic facts)

- Oklahoma's state resource lists **38** federally recognized tribes headquartered in the state; an older Indian Health Service map lists **39**. The count varies by source and date. (§8.)
- Plains and Southern Plains nations with Oklahoma headquarters include: Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma; Comanche Nation; Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes (one entity, headquarters at Concho); the Apache Tribe of Oklahoma (Plains Apache, Anadarko) and the Fort Sill Apache Tribe (Apache, Oklahoma); the Wichita and Affiliated Tribes; the Caddo Nation. (§8.)
- Sixty-seven tribes were moved into Indian Territory, which became the state of Oklahoma. (§8.)
- Kiowa, Comanche, Apache, Fort Sill Apache, Caddo, Wichita and Delaware share a joint-use area (the Kiowa–Comanche–Apache–Fort Sill–Caddo–Wichita–Delaware JUA). (§8.)

## 4. The one diorama lead found (not confirmed)

- **Southern Plains Indian Museum, Anadarko.** Its collection covers Caddo, Chiricahua Apache, Comanche, Delaware Nation, Kiowa, Plains Apache, Southern Arapaho, Southern Cheyenne and Wichita peoples. It opened on 2 December 1948. (§8.)
- A diorama of **Cheyenne people preparing to move camp** is in its collection. Figures are beeswax, hand-painted; the ground is real sand and sandstone. (§8.)
- **Date conflict:** one catalogue record says the Indian Arts and Crafts Board commissioned it in **1949**; a Google Arts & Culture story credits Allan Houser with the diorama and dates the commission to **1950**. The 1949 date is unconfirmed. Check the museum's own records before quoting it.
- **Use in this project:** this is a reference for what a real museum diorama of Plains life looked like and how it was made. It is not a source for the sample's shapes. Look at the piece itself, and ask the museum, before any copy of its composition.

## 9. The specific version (round 3): Southern Cheyenne camp move

The sample now depicts a **Southern Cheyenne camp move on the North Fork of the Canadian, Indian Territory, in the 1870s**. It is a draft for consultation. The Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes have not reviewed it. See `research/consultation-plan.md` before any display.

Facts the story rests on (claims in `exhibit/story.json`):

- **Treaty, 1867.** The Medicine Lodge Treaty was signed on 28 October 1867. It set lands in Indian Territory for the Southern Cheyenne and Arapaho, and allowed hunting only while bison remained. Source: National Park Service, https://home.nps.gov/fols/learn/historyculture/medicine-lodge-treaty.htm (claim c6, certainty 70; sources differ on the exact hunting clause).
- **Reservation, 1869.** An executive order of 10 August 1869 set aside land on the North Fork of the Canadian River, in western Indian Territory. The tribes had not lived on the land described in the 1867 treaty. Sources: 15 Stat. 593 (https://oms.library.okstate.edu/s/oklahoma-built/item/19033) and a Wikipedia summary to check (claim c5, certainty 75).
- **Bison, late 1860s and 1870s.** Bison were nearly gone from the southern Plains by then. Sources describe the decline; the exact dates vary (claim c7, certainty 60).
- **Horses and travois.** After horses came to the Plains, travois were drawn by horses and could be longer and carry more, which allowed larger lodges. Source: The Canadian Encyclopedia, https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/index.php/en/article/travois (claims c1 and c3, certainty 65).
- **Women's work in moving camp.** Women took down lodges, packed the travois and led the horses. The source describes Ute camp moves in Colorado; it is applied to the Cheyenne camp by analogy, and must be checked (claim c2, certainty 60). Source: https://www.unco.edu/hewit/doing-history/colorado-indians/work-tools/womens-work.aspx
- **The grouping of people walking beside the horses** is a scene choice, not a source (claim c4, certainty 30).

Not shown: any named person; any particular family or event; faces, hair, clothing or regalia; ceremonial or sacred objects; any nation other than the Southern Cheyenne.
